The five pure vowels

Japanese has exactly five vowel sounds, and each one stays pure and unchanging for its full duration — unlike English vowels, which often glide into a second sound (the English "o" in "go" actually glides toward "oo"). Keeping each Japanese vowel flat and unchanging is one of the single biggest improvements you can make for clear pronunciation in singing.

a
ah, as in "father"
i
ee, as in "see"
u
oo, unrounded lips, as in "food" but flatter
e
eh, as in "bed"
o
oh, as in "go" but held flat, no "w" glide at the end

Mora-timing: why Japanese lyrics feel different to sing

English is stress-timed: some syllables get stretched, others get swallowed, depending on emphasis. Japanese is mora-timed: each mora, roughly one kana character, gets close to equal rhythmic weight. A long vowel (two morae) should sound almost exactly twice as long as a short one, and a small tsu (small っ), a held silent beat, takes up a full mora of time too.

When singing, melody and mora-timing interact: a composer typically assigns one note per mora. Giving every mora its proper duration, rather than rushing through unstressed-feeling syllables the way you would in English, is what makes Japanese lyrics sound clear and idiomatic rather than hurried.

Common pronunciation pitfalls for English speakers

  • Devoicing habits leaking into singing. In spoken Japanese, i and u are often nearly whispered between voiceless consonants (such as in です, desu). In singing, these vowels are usually voiced fully to carry the melody — don't swallow them the way casual speech does.
  • Diphthongizing o and e. English speakers often glide "o" toward "ow" and "e" toward "ay." Keep both flat and pure for the entire note.
  • The Japanese r. It is a quick flap of the tongue tip against the ridge behind your teeth — closer to a light "d" or the American English "tt" in "butter" than to an English r or l. It should never be rolled.
  • Double consonants (small tsu, っ). This marks a brief, silent held stop before the next consonant, not a doubled sound. In ちょっと (chotto), there is a true beat of silence before the "to."
  • Long vowels. A long vowel, written with a doubled vowel kana or a dash in katakana, must be held for a full extra mora. Cutting it short changes both rhythm and, often, the word's meaning.
  • The syllabic n (ん). This is its own mora with its own beat, not just a consonant tacked onto the previous syllable — give it real duration rather than clipping it.
